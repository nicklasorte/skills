# Made to Stick — Briefing Playbook (v2, checked against the book)

Reference for turning technical topics into briefings that non-technical audiences (executives, decision-makers, the public) understand, remember, and repeat.

Source: Chip Heath & Dan Heath, *Made to Stick: Why Some Ideas Survive and Others Die* (Random House, 2007). The example catalog was checked against the full text. Summaries are paraphrased, not quoted. Page numbers are approximate (±1). Check exact wording in the book before quoting anything on a slide.

---

## 1. How to use this document

When running a topic through this playbook, Claude should:

1. **Fill the Topic Intake** (Section 8) from the source material.
2. **Name the Curse of Knowledge risks**: what the analyst knows that the audience doesn't (Section 3).
3. **Score the current version** against SUCCESs (Section 9).
4. **Diagnose with the Communication Framework** (Section 5): where does the message fail — attention, understanding, belief, caring, or action?
5. **Generate options per principle**, borrowing from the example catalog (Section 4) and reusable patterns (Section 7).
6. **Propose a slide sequence** (Section 10) with a principle tag on every slide.
7. **Run the guardrails** (Section 12).
8. **Stop at a confirmation gate.** Present the plan; build only after approval.

## 2. The model in one page: SUCCESs

| Principle | One-line definition | Diagnostic question |
|---|---|---|
| **S**imple | Find the core, then make it compact. Simple = core + compact. | Could a non-expert repeat the main point in one sentence? |
| **U**nexpected | Break a schema to get attention; open a curiosity gap to keep it. | What does the audience assume that is wrong? What will they want to know next? |
| **C**oncrete | Specific people doing specific things; things you can sense. | Could someone draw it? Is there a real named thing? |
| **C**redible | Let the idea carry its own credentials, ideally ones the audience can test. | Can the audience check it themselves? |
| **E**motional | Make people care: individuals, associations, self-interest, identity. | Who is affected, and why does this audience care? |
| **S**tories | Simulation (what to do) and inspiration (energy to do it). | Is there a protagonist, an obstacle, and a turn? |

Book's framing: sticky = understood, remembered, and effective at changing thought or behavior. The final lowercase "s" in SUCCESs is a joke; there are six principles.

---

## 3. The villain: the Curse of Knowledge

**Idea.** Once you know something, you can't easily imagine not knowing it. Experts drift to abstraction and to their own vocabulary, and stop noticing that the audience lacks the background.

**Key book examples**
- **Tappers and listeners** (Intro, p. ~20). Tappers drum a famous song's rhythm and predict listeners will name it about half the time. Listeners get roughly 1 in 40. The tapper hears the melody; the listener hears knocking.
- **Engineers vs. manufacturers** (Concrete, p. ~114). When something went wrong on the factory floor, engineers fixed their drawings instead of walking over to the machine. Abstraction is the expert's default.
- **"Maximize shareholder value"** (used throughout). A goal that is accurate and complete but useless for guiding a frontline decision.

**Common symptoms.** Acronyms and field jargon on slides. Leading with method instead of outcome. Equations used as explanations. Big numbers with no reference point.

**Antidote.** Use the six principles deliberately, and test on someone outside the field.

## 4. Example catalog, by chapter

Format: **Name** (page) — what happened (paraphrased) → **lesson** → *how we could borrow it*.

### Introduction: What Sticks?
- **Kidney Heist** (p. ~3) — The urban legend of a traveler drugged and waking in an ice-filled bathtub minus a kidney. Untrue, but retold for years. → Sticky ideas share traits: unexpected, concrete, emotional, a story.
- **Movie popcorn** (p. ~7) — CSPI found a medium theater popcorn had more saturated fat than a day's worth of greasy meals, and presented it as a side-by-side table of familiar foods rather than as grams. → A number becomes vivid when compared with things people know.
- **Halloween candy sadists** (p. ~14) — Widespread fear of poisoned treats with almost no real cases behind it. → Sticky doesn't mean true; truth needs good design to compete.
- **Tappers and listeners** (p. ~20) — See Section 3.
- **Systematic creativity / ad templates** (p. ~22) — Research found that award-winning ads mostly follow a handful of templates. → Creativity can be systematic; use proven patterns.
- **"What sticks" and The Tipping Point** (p. ~13) — The Heaths borrowed the term from Malcolm Gladwell; his book covers how ideas spread, theirs covers how to design the idea itself.

### Chapter 1: Simple
- **Commander's Intent** (p. ~25) — The Army learned that no plan survives contact with reality, so orders state the intent, the goal that guides improvisation. → The core is the one goal people can act on when the plan breaks.
- **"THE low-fare airline"** (p. ~29) — Southwest's core lets an employee say no to adding a popular salad to a flight. → A good core settles everyday trade-offs.
- **Burying the lead / inverted pyramid** (p. ~31) — News stories put the most important fact first. → Lead with what matters most.
- **"It's the economy, stupid"** (p. ~34) — The 1992 campaign's discipline against chasing every issue. → Defend the core against good-but-secondary points.
- **Decision paralysis** (p. ~35) — Studies where adding options leads people to choose nothing. → Too many caveats stall action.
- **The Hawaii vacation study** (p. ~36) — Students who didn't yet know an exam result paid to postpone a cheap vacation decision, even though they'd buy it whether they passed or failed. → Uncertainty, even irrelevant uncertainty, freezes decisions; remove it.
- **"If an atomic bomb fell on Raleigh…"** (p. ~44) — A fellow small-town publisher's joke that it wouldn't be news unless ashes fell on his town. → Exaggeration can make a core vivid.
- **Clinic: Sun exposure** (p. ~38) — A dry list of precautions vs. a message framed around skin aging. → Reframe around what the audience already cares about.
- **"Names, names, and names"** (p. ~43) — A small-town paper (the *Daily Record*, Dunn, NC) wins by obsessive local focus. → Commit to the core even when it costs something.
- **Proverbs** (p. ~46) — Compact statements that guide complex decisions. → Simple = core + compact.
- **The Palm Pilot wood block** (p. ~49) — The designer carried a block of wood to keep the product small and simple. → A physical stand-in keeps a team on the core.
- **Using what's there** (p. ~52) — A memory exercise: letter strings are hard to recall until they spell familiar acronyms. → Hook new information onto existing knowledge.
- **The pomelo schema** (p. ~54) — "Like a grapefruit, but larger, with a softer rind" beats a botanical description. → Explain the new through the familiar.
- **High-concept pitches** (p. ~58) — "Jaws on a spaceship" (Alien), "Die Hard on a bus" (Speed) and similar pitches carry a whole movie. → A compact analogy can carry a complex idea.
- **Generative analogies: Disney "cast members"** (p. ~61) — Treating employees as performers in a show tells them how to behave in situations no manual covers. → Choose analogies that generate good decisions.

### Chapter 2: Unexpected
- **The flight safety announcement** (p. ~63) — A flight attendant (Karen Wood) reworked the routine safety talk with humor, and passengers listened and even applauded. → Break the pattern to get attention.
- **The "Enclave" minivan ad** (p. ~67) — What looks like a car ad ends in a sudden crash, a seat-belt PSA. → Surprise must point at the core message.
- **The surprise brow** (p. ~67) — Surprise has a universal facial expression that widens our view. → Surprise is a biological attention trigger.
- **Gimmickry and "postdictability"** (p. ~71) — Good surprise feels obvious after the reveal; gimmicks don't connect to the message. → Make it surprising in advance, inevitable in hindsight.
- **Hension and phraug** (p. ~71) — A word-recognition task: nonsense words that look odd but sound familiar trigger an "oh!" → Surprise comes from a schema failing.
- **Tire chains at Nordstrom** (p. ~74) — Stories of "Nordies" going beyond service norms, including refunding tire chains the store never sold. → Unexpected concrete stories teach a value better than a policy.
- **"No school next Thursday"** (p. ~76) — Nora Ephron's journalism teacher: the real lead of a faculty-meeting story is that school is canceled. → Find what's genuinely news to the audience.
- **Clinic: Foreign aid** (p. ~78) — Most Americans overestimate foreign aid spending; the stronger message shows the real share. → Confront the specific misconception.
- **The mystery of Saturn's rings** (p. ~81) — Robert Cialdini found the best science writing posed a mystery and walked through rival explanations. → Use mystery structure for complex material.
- **Turning points in screenplays** (p. ~83) — Movies keep audiences by repeatedly opening questions. → Plant questions to hold attention.
- **Pokémon fans and car buffs** (p. ~84) — Not every interest is a mystery; the common thread is a felt gap in knowledge. → Curiosity comes from gaps, not just suspense.
- **Gap theory of curiosity** (p. ~85) — George Loewenstein: curiosity is the discomfort of a known gap. → Show the gap before filling it.
- **Clinic: Fund-raising** (p. ~87) — A board update reframed as a puzzle about why young patrons don't donate. → Even internal reports can open with a question.
- **Battling overconfidence** (p. ~89) — People think they know more than they do; making them commit to a guess first opens the gap. → Ask the room to predict before revealing.
- **NCAA football** (p. ~90) — Roone Arledge's ABC built interest in games between distant teams by first giving viewers context about the players and stakes. → Gaps need some knowledge first; prime it.
- **Pocketable radio and man on the moon** (p. ~94) — Sony's goal of a radio small enough for a pocket, and JFK's moon goal. Both were unexpected, concrete, and gave a clear finish line. → An ambitious concrete goal coordinates thousands of people.

### Chapter 3: Concrete
- **Sour grapes** (p. ~98) — Aesop's fables lasted for millennia because abstract lessons ride on concrete images.
- **Landscapes as eco-celebrities** (p. ~101) — The Nature Conservancy stopped talking in acres and started protecting specific named "landscapes." → Name the specific thing.
- **Mount Hamilton Wilderness** (p. ~103) — TNC named a stretch of unprotected hills near Silicon Valley so donors could picture it. → A name makes an abstraction protectable.
- **Teaching subtraction** (p. ~105) — Asian math teachers anchored abstract operations in concrete scenarios (buying a notebook with yen). → Concrete examples build abstract understanding.
- **Soap-opera accounting** (p. ~107) — An accounting course built around a running drama of two friends starting a business. → Concepts are discovered in context, not just defined.
- **Velcro theory of memory** (p. ~110) — Memories have many hooks (images, sounds, feelings); concrete ideas attach to more of them. Try it: recalling a state capital, a song's first line, the Mona Lisa, and a definition each feel different.
- **Brown eyes, blue eyes** (p. ~113) — Teacher Jane Elliott, after MLK's assassination, split her class by eye color to let them experience discrimination. Students remembered it for decades. → Experience beats explanation.
- **Engineers vs. manufacturers** (p. ~114) — See Section 3.
- **Boeing 727 design goal** (p. ~117) — A goal specified by passenger count, route, and runway aligned thousands of engineers. → Concrete goals allow coordination.
- **The Ferraris go to Disney World** (p. ~117) — Instead of a slide deck, a consulting firm (Stone Yamashita) built a walk-through exhibit following a fictional family's Disney vacation, showing how one company's technologies could work together. → Make strategy something people can walk through.
- **White things** (p. ~120) — Listing "white things" is harder than listing "white things in your refrigerator." → Concrete prompts unlock better thinking.
- **The leather computer** (p. ~121) — Jerry Kaplan pitched a pen-based computer to Kleiner Perkins by tossing a leather folder on the table as a model of the future. → A prop can make a future idea real.
- **Clinic: Oral rehydration therapy** (p. ~124) — Statistics vs. a concrete description of a cheap, simple treatment saving a child. → Specifics move people. James Grant of UNICEF carried a packet of salt and sugar to show heads of state that the treatment cost less than a cup of tea. → A prop beats a statistic.
- **Hamburger Helper and Saddleback Sam** (p. ~127) — Marketers visiting real families' kitchens; a church that defines its target as a detailed persona, "Saddleback Sam." → Decide for a specific person.

### Chapter 4: Credible
- **The Nobel-winning scientist no one believed** (p. ~130) — Barry Marshall and Robin Warren found that bacteria cause ulcers but couldn't get believed until Marshall drank the bacteria himself. → Credibility can require the audience to see it, not just hear it.
- **Flesh-eating bananas** (p. ~133) — A false e-mail scare spread by citing an official-sounding institute and the FDA, plus vivid detail. → Details and authority drive belief, for good or ill.
- **Authority and antiauthority: Pam Laffin** (p. ~136) — A young mother dying of smoking-related illness was more persuasive than any expert. → Lived experience is a credential.
- **The Doe Fund's driver** (p. ~137) — A formerly homeless man drove visiting funders and told his story on the way; they found him more convincing than any director. → Let the person who lived it make the case.
- **The dancing seventy-three-year-old** (p. ~138) — A dance company silenced skeptics by naming one specific older member who joined with no experience. → One vivid detail beats a general claim.
- **Jurors and the Darth Vader toothbrush** (p. ~139) — In a mock trial, irrelevant but vivid details changed verdicts. → Vivid detail raises credibility.
- **Nuclear warheads as BBs** (p. ~143) — Beyond War dropped one BB into a bucket (Hiroshima), then poured in thousands (the world's arsenal). → Turn a statistic into a sensory relationship.
- **The human-scale principle** (p. ~144) — Restate a stunning precision as something everyday. → Translate numbers to human scale.
- **Officemates as a soccer team** (p. ~145) — Stephen Covey turned survey percentages into a soccer team where few players know which goal is theirs. → Put statistics in a familiar scene.
- **Clinic: Shark attack hysteria** (p. ~149) — Rare shark attacks are compared with more mundane dangers to recalibrate fear. → Use comparison to correct a misperception.
- **The Sinatra Test: Safexpress** (p. ~152) — An Indian logistics firm won skeptical clients by citing its hardest jobs: secure distribution of a Harry Potter release and board exams. → One "if we can do it there" example is enough.
- **Edible fabric** (p. ~155) — Bill McDonough and Michael Braungart helped a Swiss textile maker (Rohner Textil) eliminate toxic chemicals. Of thousands of dye chemicals screened, only a few dozen passed, but they made a full fabric line. Swiss inspectors thought their instruments were broken because the factory's outflow was cleaner than the inflow, and costs fell. → Combines all three internal credibility tools: vivid detail, a statistic expressed as a relationship, and a Sinatra-grade case.
- **Where's the beef?** (p. ~156) — Wendy's ad invited viewers to look at a competitor's tiny patty in a huge bun. → Testable credential: the audience checks for themselves.
- **Testable credentials** (p. ~158) — Includes Reagan's "better off than four years ago" debate question and the Snapple rumor (debunked by checking the label). → Hand people the check.
- **The Emotional Tank** (p. ~158) — A youth-coaching program has coaches deliver put-downs and then praise to each other, so they feel the effect of feedback firsthand. → Let people experience the claim.
- **Clinic: Our flawed intuition** (p. ~162) — Teaching decision biases by having readers fall for one first. → Let the audience test themselves.
- **NBA rookie camp** (p. ~163) — The league taught rookies about AIDS risk by staging the encounter with women at their hotel, then revealing the women's HIV status. → A testable, experienced lesson sticks.

### Chapter 5: Emotional
- **The Mother Teresa principle: Rokia** (p. ~165) — Donors gave more to help one named girl than in response to statistics, and adding statistics to her story reduced giving. → One person beats aggregates; analytical framing can dampen feeling.
- **Beating smoking with the Truth** (p. ~171) — An anti-smoking campaign that channeled teens' anger at manipulation by tobacco companies. → Tap the emotion the audience already has.
- **Semantic stretch: "unique"** (p. ~172) — Overused words lose force. → Avoid worn emphasis words.
- **Piggybacking on associations: Rashomon and relativity** (p. ~172) — A review tied a film to Einstein's theory to borrow its prestige. → Ideas can borrow existing emotions and associations, but overuse drains them.
- **The Analytical Hat** (p. ~167) — Donors asked to do a calculation before reading about Rokia gave about half as much. → Analytical framing dampens caring; don't mix the appeal with a math exercise.
- **Reclaiming "sportsmanship"** (p. ~175) — Reframed as "honoring the game" to restore its meaning. → Rename to restore emotional force.
- **Mail-order ads: John Caples** (p. ~178) — "They laughed when I sat down at the piano…" sold through self-interest and an underdog story. → Lead with the benefit. Mail-order ads are a laboratory because, unlike a tube of Crest, every sale traces to one ad.
- **WIIFY** (p. ~178) — "What's in it for you." → Self-interest motivates.
- **Proposition 13 and self-interest in voting** (p. ~189) — Self-interest predicts votes when the effect is large, tangible, and immediate; otherwise group identity matters more. → Know which lever applies.
- **Cable TV in Tempe** (p. ~181) — Homeowners asked to imagine themselves enjoying cable signed up far more than those given a list of benefits. → Help people picture themselves benefiting.
- **Avoiding Maslow's basement** (p. ~183) — We assume others are moved by low-level needs, while we're moved by higher ones (learning, meaning). → Appeal to higher motives too.
- **Dining in Iraq: Floyd Lee** (p. ~187) — The manager of the Pegasus mess hall in Iraq saw his job as morale, not food. → Meaning drives performance.
- **The popcorn popper and political science** (p. ~188) — A marketer selling a fire-safety film wrongly assumed firefighters wanted the free popcorn popper. → Don't underestimate higher motives.
- **Clinic: Why study algebra?** (p. ~193) — Compares a definition-style answer, a self-interest answer, and one framed around higher motives. → Find the reason the audience actually cares about.
- **Don't mess with Texas** (p. ~196) — After research by Dan Syrek identified the typical litterer, anti-litter ads featuring Texas icons appealed to the identity of young, proud Texans who littered. → Identity: "what would someone like me do?"
- **Who cares about duo piano?** (p. ~200) — Arts leaders asking "why" repeatedly to find what makes their work matter to others. → Ask "why" until you hit what people care about.
- **Creating empathy** (p. ~202) — Design firm IDEO restores empathy through specific individuals. → Empathy comes from the particular.

### Chapter 6: Stories
- **The day the heart monitor lied** (p. ~205) — An experienced NICU nurse trusted her own exam over a misleading monitor and saved a newborn. → Stories carry tacit judgment.
- **Shop talk at Xerox** (p. ~207) — Copier repair technicians learned through war stories over lunch. → Stories transmit expertise.
- **Helpful and unhelpful visualizations** (p. ~209) — UCLA students who visualized the process of solving a problem did better than those who visualized the outcome. → Simulate the steps, not just success.
- **Stories as flight simulators** (p. ~211) — Hearing a story is mental rehearsal. → Stories prepare people to act.
- **Clinic: Problem students** (p. ~216) — A bullet list of tips vs. a story of handling a hostile student. → A story conveys what a list can't.
- **Jared, the Subway dieter** (p. ~219) — A real customer's story beat the planned campaign. → Spot stories rather than invent them. The heroes of the idea story are the franchisee who noticed the article and the agency director who sent an intern to find Jared.
- **The three plots** (p. ~225) — Challenge (underdog overcomes), Connection (bridging a gap between people), Creativity (a breakthrough).
  - **Challenge:** Rose Blumkin, who arrived with no English and built a furniture store into a $100 million business and worked the floor at 100; David and Goliath; Shackleton.
  - **Connection:** the Good Samaritan (a stranger from a hostile group helps); the Mean Joe Greene Coke ad; Romeo and Juliet.
  - **Creativity:** the Ingersoll-Rand "Drag Test," where a team settled a materials question by dragging samples behind a rental car instead of running months of lab tests.
- **Springboard stories at the World Bank** (p. ~232) — Stephen Denning used a brief story of a Zambian health worker finding information online to help leaders imagine knowledge management. → A short true story lets the audience extrapolate.
- **How to make presenters angry with stories** (p. ~236) — Gary Klein's team summarized a conference by collecting the stories presenters told. The organizer loved it; the presenters were upset, because they wanted to be remembered for their abstract recommendations. → Audiences remember the stories, even when experts value the abstractions.

### Epilogue: What Sticks
- **Nice guys finish last** (p. ~238) — Durocher's actual quote evolved into a simpler, more universal one. → Ideas get simplified in retelling; design for that.
- **"Elementary, my dear Watson"** (p. ~239) — Holmes never said it. → Audiences compress ideas to fit their schemas.
- **The power of spotting** (p. ~241) — Recognize sticky ideas already in your environment.
- **The speakers and the stickers** (p. ~243) — In Chip's Stanford class, students give one-minute speeches. Afterward, almost all listeners remember the stories and almost none remember the statistics, though most speakers used statistics. → Polished delivery doesn't make ideas stick.
- **More villains / Curse of Knowledge again** (p. ~245).
- **John F. Kennedy vs. Floyd Lee** (p. ~251) — You don't need JFK's charisma. Floyd Lee's mess hall shows ordinary people can make ideas stick. → The principles are learnable.

## 5. Communication framework (Epilogue)

| Stage | Principle that helps |
|---|---|
| 1. Pay attention | Unexpected |
| 2. Understand and remember | Concrete |
| 3. Agree / believe | Credible |
| 4. Care | Emotional |
| 5. Be able to act | Story |
| (throughout) | Simple, mainly in shaping the core |

**Symptom → fix** (paraphrased from the epilogue's troubleshooting):
- **"No one is listening"** → break a schema, open a gap (Unexpected).
- **"They don't get it" / "they forget"** → concrete examples, analogies, specifics (Concrete).
- **"They don't believe it"** → details, human-scale stats, Sinatra example, testable credentials (Credible).
- **"They don't care"** → individuals, associations, self-interest, identity (Emotional).
- **"They don't act"** → stories that simulate and inspire (Stories).

```
BIG NUMBERS NEEDING A RELATIONSHIP:
WHO IS AFFECTED, AND HOW:

HARDEST CASE (Sinatra candidate):

SENSITIVITIES:
JARGON TO REMOVE OR DEFINE:
```

## 9. Scoring rubric (0–2 per principle)

| Principle | 0 | 1 | 2 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Simple | No clear core, or several competing | Core exists but is buried or long | One-sentence core, stated first |
| Unexpected | Nothing challenges expectations | Surprise unrelated to the core | Surprise opens a gap that the core closes |
| Concrete | Abstract, jargon | Some specifics | Real, named, visual anchor throughout |
| Credible | Assertion or authority only | Data, but not human-scale | Testable by the audience, or a Sinatra case |
| Emotional | No person or consequence | Generic stakes | A named role or place the audience cares about |
| Stories | Static facts | Sequence without a protagonist | Protagonist, obstacle, turn, outcome |

Target: at least 1 on every principle across the briefing, and 2 on Simple and Concrete.

## 10. Briefing sequence template

1. **Hook** (Unexpected / Credible): a relationship, not a number.
2. **Stakes** (Emotional): who is affected and how.
3. **Core message** (Simple): one sentence, alone on the slide.
4. **Meet the protagonist** (Concrete): one named real thing.
5. **How it works, through the protagonist** (Concrete): the method on one case.
6. **Scale** (Concrete): one to many, anchored on the protagonist.
7. **The journey** (Stories): episodes, simulating the process.
8. **The twist** (Unexpected): a plausible answer that fails, then the right one.
9. **Check it yourself** (Credible): testable credential, plus honest pending items.
10. **Hardest case → everywhere** (Credible / Sinatra): close with the asks.

Method detail and equations go in backup.

## 11. Output format for a run

```
1. Intake (Section 8)
2. Curse-of-knowledge risks (3–5 bullets)
3. Framework diagnosis (Section 5): where the message breaks down
4. Before score (Section 9)
5. Options by principle (2–3 each, tied to a catalog example or pattern)
6. Proposed sequence (Section 10), with principle tags
7. Accuracy check: every claim traced to source; illustrations labeled
8. After score (projected)
9. Open questions for the user (confirmation gate)
```

## 12. Guardrails

- **Accuracy beats stickiness.** The book's Halloween and Kidney Heist examples are warnings: false ideas stick too. Never simplify a result past what the analysis supports.
- **Label illustrations.** Invented examples, sample values, and schematic curves are marked "Illustration" on slides and explained in notes.
- **Say what's pending.** Placeholder values, unverified inputs, and missing data are stated plainly, which builds credibility.
- **Keep math checkable.** Every derived number must be reproducible from stated inputs.
- **Sensitivity.** Respect release limits on locations, names, and data precision for wider audiences.
- **Project rules.** Apply any standing project rules or constraints during the accuracy check.
- **Quoting the book.** Summaries here are paraphrased. Check exact wording and page before quoting on a slide.
